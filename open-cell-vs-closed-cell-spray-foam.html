Open-Cell vs. Closed-Cell Spray Foam: Which Is Right for Your San Diego Home?

Two foams, two very different jobs. Here's how to tell which one your project actually needs.

Updated February 2026 6 min read

If you've started researching spray foam, you've hit the first big decision fast: open-cell or closed-cell? Both are excellent insulation, both are sprayed on-site, and both dramatically outperform old fiberglass batts. But they behave differently, cost differently, and shine in different parts of your home. Here's the plain-English breakdown.

The 30-second version

Open-cell foam is lighter, softer, and less expensive. It's a fantastic value for interior walls and attics, and it's the better sound-dampener. Closed-cell foam is denser and more expensive, but it delivers a higher R-value per inch, adds structural rigidity, and acts as its own moisture barrier — making it the go-to for crawl spaces, roofs, and anywhere humidity is a concern.

Rule of thumb for San Diego: open-cell for comfort and budget in conditioned spaces; closed-cell where moisture, tight spaces, or structural strength matter most.

R-value: how much insulating power per inch

R-value measures resistance to heat flow — higher is better. This is where the two foams diverge the most:

  • Open-cell: roughly R-3.5 to R-3.8 per inch.
  • Closed-cell: roughly R-6.5 to R-7 per inch.

Closed-cell packs nearly twice the insulating value into the same thickness. That matters when you have limited depth to work with — a shallow roof rafter, for example — where you simply can't fit enough open-cell to hit the target R-value.

Moisture: San Diego's coastal factor

Homes near the coast — Pacific Beach, Point Loma, Carlsbad, Encinitas — deal with marine humidity year-round. Closed-cell foam is a Class II vapor retarder, meaning it resists water intrusion and won't absorb moisture. That's why we almost always specify closed-cell for crawl spaces, subfloors, and any assembly touching the outside air near the coast.

Open-cell, by contrast, is vapor-permeable. In the right interior application that's perfectly fine (and even helpful for letting assemblies dry), but it's not what you want as a moisture barrier.

Sound control

Here's where open-cell wins. Its softer, more porous structure absorbs airborne sound better, which makes it a great choice for interior walls between bedrooms, home offices, media rooms, or any home near a busy street or flight path. If a quieter house is high on your list, open-cell in the interior walls is money well spent.

Cost

Open-cell generally costs less per board foot because it uses less raw material to fill the same space. Closed-cell costs more, but you're paying for higher performance and added benefits. For many San Diego homes, the smartest budget is a mix: closed-cell where it counts, open-cell where it's plenty.

So which should you choose?

Choose open-cell if you're insulating interior walls or an attic in a conditioned space, want the best value, and care about sound. Choose closed-cell if you're dealing with crawl spaces, subfloors, roofs, coastal moisture, tight cavities, or want a little extra structural strength. Honestly? Most homes benefit from a thoughtful combination — and that's exactly the kind of recommendation we make for free at your estimate.
